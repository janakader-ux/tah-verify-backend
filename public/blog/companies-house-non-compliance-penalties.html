What Happens If You Don't Verify Your Identity with Companies House?

Compliance 4 February 2026 · 8 min read · By Kader Ameen, EA, FMAAT

Most directors treat Companies House identity verification as paperwork — something to tick off alongside the confirmation statement and move on. That reading understates it considerably. Under the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023, identity verification is a standalone legal duty, and failing to meet it is a criminal matter, not an administrative one. This article sets out, in plain terms and with the underlying legal sources, exactly what happens if you don't verify — and why "I'll get to it" is a riskier position than most directors realise.

The legal duty, in two steps

According to Companies House's published approach to non-compliance, identity verification has two distinct steps: completing a successful identity check to obtain a Companies House personal code, and then filing a verification statement that supplies that code to Companies House. Completing the first step without the second does not satisfy the law — both steps must happen. Directors, LLP members, general partners, managing officers, PSCs, and ACSPs are all named as people who must verify.

It is a criminal offence to act as an unverified director

Companies House states plainly that "it is unlawful for a director to act as a director without completing identity verification." This isn't a late-filing penalty in the way a missed accounts deadline is — it sits alongside offences under the Companies Act 2006. Even where Companies House does not select a case for prosecution, the same guidance confirms that a director "may still be committing an offence under section 167M of the Companies Act for continuing to act without identity verification," and that the company itself may also be in breach if one of its directors or equivalents remains unverified (GOV.UK). PSCs who fail to verify may also be committing an offence in their own right.

What enforcement actually looks like

Companies House has been explicit that it will take a "fair and proportionate" approach rather than pursuing every late submission as a criminal case (Companies House, January 2026). But "proportionate" does not mean toothless. Its published enforcement toolkit includes:

  • A default letter setting out the specific offence committed and warning that further action may follow without additional notice.
  • Financial penalties, which can be issued at company level and at individual officer level.
  • Annotating the public register to show non-compliance — visible to anyone searching the company, including banks, landlords and suppliers running due diligence.
  • Referral to The Insolvency Service for directors who continue to act while unverified.
  • Prosecution through the courts, which can lead to a criminal conviction, director disqualification, and fines.
  • Director disqualification under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986, and ultimately striking the company off the register.

Companies House has indicated it is more likely to treat a case as "serious" — and therefore prioritise it for prosecution — where a person or company has committed three or more offences over a five-year period, though it describes this as a guideline rather than a fixed rule (GOV.UK).

The immediate practical problem: you can't file

Long before any letter or penalty arrives, most directors hit a far more immediate wall: Companies House will not accept a confirmation statement while any of the company's directors remain unverified. Since 18 November 2025, confirmation statements are filed together with each director's personal code and verification statement, and industry guidance is consistent that one unverified director blocks the whole filing (Moving to the UK, 2026; ICAEW). A late confirmation statement is itself a separate compliance failure, so an unverified director can cascade into a filing default for the whole company — well before Companies House gets anywhere near a prosecution decision.

If you have a genuine reason for delay

Companies House does allow for extenuating circumstances through a formal "representation." If accepted, enforcement action is typically paused for up to two months to allow you to put things right (GOV.UK). The published example of an accepted representation is a company experiencing a serious IT failure at the exact point the verification statement was due, with no alternative route available. Representations submitted after the relevant deadline will not be considered, and once decided, the outcome is final — so this is a safety net for genuine emergencies, not a way to buy time.

The sensible approach

Given the stakes — a personal criminal offence, potential disqualification, and a blocked confirmation statement — the practical advice from accountants and company secretarial practitioners is consistent: verify as early in your transition window as possible, rather than waiting for the deadline tied to your next confirmation statement. If you're not confident navigating GOV.UK One Login yourself, or you're managing several directorships with different filing dates, an Authorised Corporate Service Provider like we can check your documents, manage the submission, and confirm your Companies House code has been correctly linked — removing the guesswork.

Key takeaways

  • Acting as a director without a completed identity verification is a criminal offence, not a late-filing fee.
  • Companies House's enforcement toolkit spans default letters, financial penalties, public register annotation, disqualification and prosecution.
  • In practice, an unverified director blocks the company's entire confirmation statement — the practical consequence usually lands before any enforcement letter does.
  • Genuine emergencies can be raised as a formal representation, but only before the deadline passes.
  • Verifying early, rather than at the last permitted moment, avoids the whole problem.
